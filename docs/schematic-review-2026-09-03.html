What the schematic is for, and how close it can honestly be

You asked two things: how a schematic is made today in each case, and what a reader actually needs from it to spot a UI bug or understand the behaviour that was wanted. The second question decides the first.

A Focus row has two pictures of the same moment. The photograph is what the app did. The schematic must be what the requirement wants, drawn in the app's own geometry. The row exists to show the difference between them. Likeness is not the goal; it is what makes the difference readable.

Everything below follows from that. A drawing that copies the photograph perfectly tells the reader nothing. A drawing that shows the intent but looks nothing like the app cannot be compared. The useful schematic is the intent wearing the app's clothes, with the disagreement marked.

Recommendation

Keep one path. Make it an intent drawing with a diff, not a twin.

1 · Mark the difference highest value

On a failed scene, draw a difference marker at the ringed element on both pictures: the expected word on the schematic, the measured word on the photograph, joined by one label ("expected Published · got Live"). Two rings, one sentence, no pixel hunting.

Cost: renderer + the proof cell's overlay. Uses the claims that already ride the skeleton. Failure mode: a beat with several soft claims needs several markers; cap it at the failed ones.

2 · A loupe on the ring highest value

Under each row, the ringed component alone, drawn and photographed, at a fixed legible size. The full page stays as context above it. This is the "extremely alike" comparison surface; the page never was.

Cost: a crop of the frame plus a re-render of the ring's neighbourhood at scale. Failure mode: a ring on a whole row loupes a row, which is fine.

3 · Show the claims on passing beats medium

Tint every element a claim touched, in the requirement's dye, even when it passed. The drawing then says what the requirement is about, and a proof that never touches the control the Then names is visibly thin.

Cost: renderer only; the values already carry their claims. Failure mode: none for meaning; a busy beat gets busy.

4 · Intent must be authored, not inferred medium

The drawing can only show intent the test claimed. Make the kg-e2e rule explicit: every fact in a Then is a claim (soft), including "appears" and "is gone", so mirror-13 has something to draw. The gate can then refuse a beat whose Then names a state no claim covers.

Cost: a lint rule on beats against their Then text. This is the one that turns the drawing from a picture of the app into a picture of the requirement.

5 · No UI yet: sketch in the app's chrome later

Draw the archetype inside a real skeleton borrowed from a sibling screen of the same app (its header, sidebar, grid style), with the new component sketched where the requirement's Given puts it. It then looks like this product, and the first harvest replaces only the sketched part.

Cost: a "borrow chrome" step in the derive pass. Honest label stays: "a sketch, no UI yet".

6 · Keep the label honest keep

A mirror is labelled a mirror, a sketch a sketch, a text-only row text-only, and a drawing behind its harvest or its text says so. This is already right and is what makes the rest trustworthy.

No change. Do not soften the gate to make a row look finished.

Order of work if you agree: 1 and 2 together (they share the ring's geometry), then 4 (a lint, cheap), then 3, then 5 when a screen without UI actually needs it. Each is a renderer or authoring change; none touches how requirements are stored or proven.

What I would argue against

Two tempting paths, and why not

  • A model-drawn schematic from the prose. It would look plausible and be unfalsifiable. Nothing could gate it against the app, so the row would show two pictures with no honest relation. Every rule this board has is about the drawing being derived, never guessed.
  • Copying the photograph into the schematic cell. It is the perfect mirror and says nothing. The value of the row is the difference; a twin has none.
